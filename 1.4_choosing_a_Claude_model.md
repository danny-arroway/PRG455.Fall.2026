# PRG455 — Choosing a Claude Model

**Companion reading to §1.3 (Understanding LLMs) · Reference throughout the course**

§1.3 explained what parameters, layers, context windows, and quantization are, in general. This
page applies those ideas to the specific tool you're using all semester: Claude, through your
Claude Pro subscription. Pro gives you access to several different Claude models, not one — and
picking the right one, and the right **effort** setting, changes both the quality of what you get
back and how fast you burn through your weekly allowance.

**As of August 2026**, this is accurate. Anthropic ships new models fairly often, so treat the
model *names and tiers* below as durable and the exact specs as a snapshot — if something looks
off partway through the term, ask in lecture and we'll check the current docs together.

## 1. The lineup, at a glance

Claude comes in four tiers, roughly ordered from fastest/cheapest to most capable:

| Tier | Current model | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Haiku** | Claude Haiku 4.5 | Speed. Simple, high-volume tasks. |
| **Sonnet** | Claude Sonnet 5 | The default. Best balance of speed and capability for most work. |
| **Opus** | Claude Opus 5 | Complex agentic coding and hard multi-step reasoning. |
| **Fable** | Claude Fable 5 | The single most capable model Anthropic offers, for the hardest tasks. |

There's also **Claude Mythos 5**, which sits alongside Fable — same underlying capability, but
restricted to an invitation-only research program (Project Glasswing) with extra safety
restrictions around biology, cybersecurity, and AI-research misuse. You won't have access to it
through Claude Pro, and you don't need it for this course.

### What each one is actually for

**Haiku — the fast one.**
Haiku trades some raw capability for speed and low cost. Think of it as the model you'd want
behind a chatbot that needs to answer instantly, or for simple, repetitive tasks like
classifying text or looking something up. For PRG455, Haiku is fine for quick syntax questions
("what's the MicroPython equivalent of X") but can be shakier on multi-step debugging or
anything that needs it to reason carefully about your actual hardware setup.

**Sonnet — the default, and the one you'll use most.**
Sonnet is built to be the best combination of speed and intelligence for everyday work — strong
at coding, capable of extended reasoning when a problem needs it, but not as slow or expensive
as the tiers above it. This is very likely the model Claude Code and claude.ai put you on by
default, and it's the right choice for the large majority of what you'll do in this course:
writing and debugging M5UI code, working through prompt specifications, reviewing your own logic.

**Opus — for when the task is genuinely hard.**
Opus is tuned for complex agentic coding and long, multi-step problems — the kind of task where
the model needs to hold a lot of context, make a plan, and follow it across many steps without
losing the thread. If you find yourself in a long back-and-forth with Sonnet that isn't
converging on a working answer — say, a subtle bug across several interacting event handlers —
that's a reasonable moment to reach for Opus instead of continuing to argue with Sonnet (though
per §2.1, restarting with a better-specified prompt is *also* worth trying first).

**Fable — the ceiling.**
Fable is Anthropic's most capable widely released model, positioned above Opus for the hardest,
highest-stakes reasoning and long-running agentic tasks. It's also the slowest and most
expensive per token. You are unlikely to need it for anything in this course — Lab 3's VRAM
formula and event-handler logic are well within Sonnet's comfort zone — but it exists if you
ever hit a problem that's genuinely stumping the other tiers.

### The practical rule of thumb

> **Start with Sonnet (the default). Move up a tier only when you have a concrete reason to —
> not as a first resort "just in case."**

Every tier above Sonnet costs more of your shared Pro allowance per message, and none of them
make ambiguous prompts less ambiguous. A vague question sent to Fable is still a vague question —
see §2.1 on precision prompting. Model choice and prompt precision are separate levers; pull the
prompt-precision one first, it's free.

## 2. What "effort" does

Separately from *which model* you pick, several current models — Sonnet 5, Opus 5, and Fable 5
among them — support an **effort** setting, and Haiku 4.5 supports **extended thinking**, a
related but distinct control. Where you'll actually encounter this as a student is inside
Claude Code, where you can set how much the model "thinks" before and during a response.

### The core idea

Effort is a **behavioral dial**, not a hard token budget. At higher effort, Claude is more
willing to:

- Pause and reason through a problem step-by-step before answering (this reasoning is called
  **adaptive thinking** — the model decides for itself, per message, whether a problem is worth
  thinking through first)
- Make more tool calls, and explain its plan before acting
- Produce more thorough explanations, more code comments, more careful double-checking

At lower effort, Claude tends to:

- Skip the step-by-step reasoning on problems that don't clearly need it
- Combine steps, make fewer tool calls, move straight to an answer
- Give terser output — less preamble, shorter explanations

**Effort is a signal, not a switch that turns reasoning fully off.** Even at a low effort
setting, Claude will still stop and think carefully if a problem is genuinely hard — it's just
less willing to do so on problems that don't obviously call for it. This matters for a course
where you're often asking small, well-specified questions: for those, low or medium effort is
usually both faster *and* just as correct as high effort, because there was never much reasoning
required in the first place.

### Why this matters for your allowance

Every extra round of internal reasoning is tokens — and as §2.1 explained, your Pro allowance is
shared across claude.ai, Claude Desktop, and Claude Code in VS Code and the terminal, running on
a rolling five-hour window plus a weekly cap. A model working at high effort on a task that
didn't need it isn't giving you a better answer, just a more expensive one.

**Higher effort is not automatically a "better" answer.** It's a trade: more tokens and more
latency, spent on more thorough reasoning and verification. For a task that's genuinely
ambiguous or has many interacting constraints — like architecting how your Lab 8 dropdown widget
should drive your Wi-Fi connection state — that trade is worth it. For "what's the correct
argument order for `slider.set_value()`," it isn't; you already know the answer is short and
factual, and high effort just spends extra tokens getting to the same one-line reply.

### The practical rule of thumb

> **Match effort to the actual difficulty of the question, not to how important the task feels.**

A lab being worth 5.5% of your grade doesn't make "how do I center a label" a hard question.
Save higher effort for the moments where the model genuinely has to reason across multiple
constraints at once — like reconciling the VRAM formula in Lab 3 with the text-wrapping
constraint from Lecture 3 §4 in a single piece of code.

## 3. Model size, in relative terms

You'll see other AI tools — and plenty of forum posts — describe models by their **parameter
count**: "an 8B model," "a 70B model," and so on, exactly as explained in §1.3.

**Anthropic does not publish parameter counts for any Claude model.** This isn't unusual —
several major AI labs treat model size as closely held information, the way a manufacturer
might not publish exactly how an engine is built even though they'll tell you its horsepower and
fuel economy. So this page can't give you a number, and you should be skeptical of any number
you see floating around online claiming to be "the" parameter count for a Claude model — it's
almost certainly a guess.

What can be said honestly, in **relative** terms:

- Claude models sit in the same general size class as the largest publicly available open-source
  models you'll encounter — the kind of model that, per §1.3's table, needs serious data-center
  hardware to run at full precision, not a single consumer GPU. They are not in the same
  category as the small, locally-runnable open models (in the roughly 1B–20B parameter range)
  that you *can* run on a laptop or a single high-end GPU, quantized or not.
- **Within** the Claude lineup, the tiers are not all the same size. The general pattern across
  the industry — and a reasonable one to assume here — is that faster, cheaper tiers (Haiku)
  achieve their speed partly through being smaller or more optimized, while the higher tiers
  (Opus, Fable) trade some of that speed for more capacity. But Anthropic doesn't confirm exact
  relative sizes between its own tiers either, so treat this as a general pattern, not a
  confirmed fact about Claude specifically.
- **Parameter count alone is a poor predictor of quality anyway.** §1.3 already covered this
  indirectly: training data quality, the SFT and RLHF process, and architectural choices all
  matter as much as raw size. A model's benchmark performance and its behavior on your actual
  task tell you far more than its parameter count would, even if that count were public.

### The practical takeaway

For this course, size comparisons to open-source models are trivia, not something you need for
your labs. What matters for PRG455 is the **tier comparison table in §1**, not a parameter
count — you're choosing based on documented speed/cost/capability trade-offs, not guessing at
model internals.

## Common questions

**Do I need to manually pick a model for this course?**
No — Sonnet, the default, is correct for nearly everything in PRG455. This page exists so you
understand *why* it's the default, and so you can make an informed choice on the rare occasion
a task genuinely calls for something else.

**Will switching models fix a bad answer?**
Sometimes — but check §2.1 first. An ambiguous prompt produces a plausible-but-wrong answer
regardless of which model you send it to. Tighten the specification before you reach for a
bigger model.

**Is a higher tier always "smarter"?**
Broadly, yes, in the sense that Opus and Fable can sustain more complex, longer chains of
reasoning than Sonnet, and Sonnet can sustain more than Haiku. But "smarter" doesn't mean
"gives a better answer to every question" — for a short factual question, all four tiers will
typically get it right, and the faster, cheaper one is the better choice.

**Where can I check the current model lineup if this page is out of date?**
Anthropic's model documentation is the source of truth; ask in lecture if you're not sure how to
find it, and we'll confirm together rather than relying on a possibly stale web search result.
